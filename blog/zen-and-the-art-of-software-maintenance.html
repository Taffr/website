Zen and the Art of Software Maintenance

An Inquiry into Values

In 1974 Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance by Robert M. Pirsig (1928 - 2017) was first published after rejection from hundreds of publishing houses. The book instantly became a best seller, selling over 50 000 copies in the first three months.

The title is almost intentionally misleading, already within the first few pages the author explains that the book "should in no way be associated with that great body of factual information relating to orthodox Zen Buddhist practice. It's not very factual on motorcycles, either." The book is actually a autobiographical novel about a father traveling with his son on a motorcycle through the pacific northwest. The father trying his best to understand his son and mend their bond which he can feel deteriorating. The author also recollects past events and educates us the readers via smaller, philosophical discussion intended for ease of digestion. These philosophical discussions, as well as the recollections revolve around epistemology, history of philosophy, and most importantly to engineers, the philosophy of science and technology and concept of Quality.

But the subject I would like to highlight is one that concerns us the most as software professionals, how we interact with technology and how it affects our quality of life.

Gumptionology

Known categories of gumption traps

Gumption is an old Scottish word, roughly translating to motivation or enthusiasm. As Pirsig puts it: "A person who is filled with gumption doesn't sit around dissipating and stewing about things. He's at the front of the train of his own awareness, watching to see what's up the track and meeting it when it comes." I hope that I don't have to convince you that this isn't something that is esoteric in any way, we've all felt this feeling, this seemingly unlimited reservoir of motivation, your brain is working five steps ahead, you are simply unstoppable. You might've not called it gumption, but that is exactly what Pirsig is describing. When interacting with technology, weather it is repairing your motorcycle or debugging a flaky test, depending on your level of gumption you will have more or less satisfaction during the process. It also essential to performing work with Quality* and avoiding mistakes.

Maintaining gumption can be seen as keeping a steady supply of "psychic gasoline", if you haven't got any gasoline in your tank you can basically give up before you start, since there is no way you are going to get anywhere anyways. However, if you do got it, and you know how to keep it, then there is no way that the problem you have set out to solve, be it repairing a motorcycle or fixing a bug, isn't going to get solved. I'd argue that maintaining a high level of gumption is what makes or breaks your workday, as such one should avoid losing it, that is stepping into what Pirsig calls "gumption traps".

Gumption Traps

"Gumption traps" are, as you might have guessed, things that drain enthusiasm and motivation, things that makes your day feel like it drags on and on, the things that makes you want to quit your job in tech and move into the woods and never touch a computer again.

You are probably running into one or more gumption traps a day, examples of which will be listed but if you are unlucky, you might even have these built in your process at your company, effectively causing you demotivation by (unintentional) design.

Pirsig goes to mention two major classes of gumption traps:
Set-Backs and Hang-Ups.

Set-Backs

Set-Backs are what also referred to as external traps, due them being caused by external circumstances, the World if you will, is pushing back against your efforts. Within the set-backs category, we have several sub categories, some of which software is more or less affected by. The subcategories are the following, with an example for motorcycle maintenence.

  • Out-of-Order Assemblies
    • Forgetting something early in the re-assembly process, and having to take it apart again. Undoing potentially hours of work.
  • Intermittent and Elusive Problems
    • Problems that only show up sometimes, or only under very specific conditions. Such as only during rain, or when running the engine at a certain temperature.
  • Parts or Material Failure
    • While performing maintenance, having a part break unexpectedly or when replacing one part breaking another. Especially if the broken one is one that is hard to come by.
As software maintainers we are spared from some of the external problems that might affect us, but only if we take time to use high quality tools. For instance, out-of-order assemblies is a concern which we in many instances choosing to use a dynamically compiled language, which will throw errors and warnings if you would forget to fix a call site when performing a refactor. And if all fails, we have version control which in essence is just time travel we have invented for ourselves. Parts or material failures is also a rare occurence for the same reasons.
Of course Out-of-Order assemblies exists outside of the code base as well, imagine if you have to, a scenario where you built a whole feature and getting ready for release, but just as you are getting ready your Product Person™, decides something should work slightly different, but just different enough that it cannot support the intended use case. This leaves you with two options, either rebuild it from scratch*, or make a low-quality hack, which Pirsig describes as "gumption desperation", which leads to more mistakes.
In general, avoiding the Out-of-Order assembly traps is about staying organized, making sure you are building the right thing by communicating with everyone involved, deferring every decision that can be deferred to the last possible moment. You'd be surprised how long you can do this and how many of those decisions never have to be made.

In contrast, Intermittent and Elusive problems are a much more technical issue. Having your code work sometimes is among the most frustrating parts of software engineering. Classic examples include but are not limited to, race-conditions, flaky tests, and issues with issues with localizations and time zones, according to Pirsig these first evolve into gumption traps when you thought you had already fixed them, but they keep showing up again and again.
When diagnosing intermittent issues, it is of the utmost importance to correlate them to what other things are happening at the same time. Are we taking backups at the same time as running scheduled jobs, overloading our database? Are the customers affected by this issue always in Poland?

Dealing with intermittent issues can be difficult. Pirsig makes an important note that if you are the one who performs the maintenance and the one who is trying to diagnose the issue, you have the advantage of being able to replicate the issues yourself. If you were to leave it to a mechanic, you'd be no better off, since they have much less context and will more than likely hand you back a still broken motorcycle that will have its issues resurface shortly again. If you have your own machine, and run it regularly for a longer time you are more likely to find the symptoms and then the cause. Similarly, we should try to run our machine as much as possible, and that means running in the same environment or at least as close to the same environment as much as possible as the environment that the intermittent issue appears in, run against real databases, emulate your cloud setup using LocalStack (or better yet MiniStack) using Docker, you might be able to find some of them when running both automated and manual testing locally. But the most important part is being able to see and correlate correctly, if you are not sure when or what is happening, you can forget about being able to figure out why. So make sure your code is observable, using traces and logs and being able to aggregate them and make sense of them.
If all else fails, and if you can tolerate it, then it might be that you might have to live with the intermittent failure for a bit. To gather more information and correlate the failures as independent incident to get a bigger picture.
