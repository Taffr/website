Zen and the Art of Software Maintenance

An Inquiry into Values

In 1974 Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance by Robert M. Pirsig (1928 - 2017) was first published after rejection from hundreds of publishing houses. The book instantly became a best seller, selling over 50 000 copies in the first three months.

The title is almost intentionally misleading, already within the first few pages the author explains that the book "should in no way be associated with that great body of factual information relating to orthodox Zen Buddhist practice. It's not very factual on motorcycles, either."

The book is a autobiographical novel about a father traveling with his son on a motorcycle through the pacific northwest. The father trying his best to understand his son and mend their . The author also recollects past events and educates us the readers via smaller, easily digested, philosophical discussions. These philosophical discussions, as well as the recollections revolve around epistemology, history of philosophy, and most importantly to engineers, the philosophy of science and technology.

But the subject I would like to highlight is one that concerns us the most as software professionals, how we interact with technology and how it affects our quality of life.

Gumptionology

Gumption is an old Scottish word, roughly translating to motivation or enthusiasm. When interacting with technology, weather it is repairing your motorcycle or debugging a flaky test, depending on your level of gumption you will have more or less satisfaction during the process. I'd argue that maintaining a high level of gumption is what makes or breaks your workday, as such one should avoid losing it, that is stepping into what Pirsig calls "gumption traps".

Gumption Traps

"Gumption traps" are, as you might have guessed, things that drain enthusiasm and motivation, things that makes your day feel like it drags on and on, the things that makes you want to quit your job in tech and move into the woods and never touch a computer again.
