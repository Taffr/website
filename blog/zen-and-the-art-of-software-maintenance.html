Zen and the Art of Software Maintenance

An Inquiry into Values

In 1974 Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance by Robert M. Pirsig (1928 - 2017) was first published after rejection from hundreds of publishing houses. The book instantly became a best seller, selling over 50 000 copies in the first three months.

The title is almost intentionally misleading, already within the first few pages the author explains that the book "should in no way be associated with that great body of factual information relating to orthodox Zen Buddhist practice. It's not very factual on motorcycles, either."

The book is a autobiographical novel about a father traveling with his son on a motorcycle through the pacific northwest. The father trying his best to understand his son and mend their . The author also recollects past events and educates us the readers via smaller, easily digested, philosophical discussions. These philosophical discussions, as well as the recollections revolve around epistemology, history of philosophy, and most importantly to engineers, the philosophy of science and technology.

But the subject I would like to highlight is one that concerns us the most as software professionals, how we interact with technology and how it affects our quality of life.

Gumptionology

Gumption is an old Scottish word, roughly translating to motivation or enthusiasm. As Pirsig puts it: "A person who is filled with gumption doesn't sit around dissipating and stewing about things. He's at the front of the train of his own awareness, watching to see what's up the track and meeting it when it comes." I hope that I don't have to convince you that this isn't something that is esoteric in any way, we've all felt this feeling, this seemingly unlimited reservoir of motivation, your brain is working five steps ahead, you are simply unstoppable. You might've not called it gumption, but that is exactly what Pirsig is describing. When interacting with technology, weather it is repairing your motorcycle or debugging a flaky test, depending on your level of gumption you will have more or less satisfaction during the process. It also essential to performing work with Quality* and avoiding mistakes. Maintaining gumption can be seen as keeping a steady supply of "psychic gasoline", if you haven't got any gasoline in your tank you can basically give up before you start, since there is no way you are going to get anywhere anyways. However, if you do got it, and you know how to keep it, then there is no way that the problem you have set out to solve, be it repairing a motorcycle or fixing a bug, isn't going to get solved. I'd argue that maintaining a high level of gumption is what makes or breaks your workday, as such one should avoid losing it, that is stepping into what Pirsig calls "gumption traps".

Gumption Traps

"Gumption traps" are, as you might have guessed, things that drain enthusiasm and motivation, things that makes your day feel like it drags on and on, the things that makes you want to quit your job in tech and move into the woods and never touch a computer again.

You are probably running into one or more gumption traps a day, examples of which will be listed but if you are unlucky, you might even have these built in your process at your company, effectively causing you demotivation by (unintentional) design.

Pirsig goes to mention two major classes of gumption traps: Set-Backs and Hang-Ups.
